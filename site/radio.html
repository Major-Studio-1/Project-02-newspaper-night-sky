PressesEach surviving issue is a grey dot and a soft wooden note, its pitch the town's latitude, south low and north high and a step of the piano's left hand, which rocks through each bar's chord and climbs higher when more newspapers print; struck, it glows. A day with no surviving issue is a rest.
StoriesA printing of a story is a ring, gold if it was checked against the scans and blue if it was found by shared wording; a plucked string sounds it; the piano's right hand sings it, by steps, higher in the busier weeks, a flute opening each rise and a clarinet answering after its peak. The papers that printed the story in the 40 days before ring with it.
StringsSoft strings hold the season's chordeach bar's chord, leaning to tension while the weeks get busier and resolving as they quieten, and the cellos join in the busiest weeks. They are fuller when more newspapers survive around now, not when there is more news.
Air and New YearA faint breath under everything, so a gap in the archive sounds like a gap, not like the music stopping; a soft druma stroke of the timpani on each 1 January. What you hear

  • The words are passages the newspapers printed on that date, as the archive transcribed them: one passage from each paper that day. Reviewed passages (1770–1783 and 1787–1810) come first; they were checked against the page scans, and their notes record misreadings, but the words read aloud are still the machine transcription (in 1776 you hear “slaves” where the papers printed “staves”). Matched passages were found automatically and not checked.
  • The order: the radio starts on today's date in the chosen year. Each day starts with a greeting to the town of the first paper. Then the radio moves to the next day with surviving pages. Days with no surviving page are skipped, which is a gap in the archive, not silence in the town.
  • The voices were recorded once, when the site was built, with Kokoro, an open speech model: two announcers who take turns, and nine readers, each newspaper keeping one. They are modern and synthetic, not voices of the time. A passage without a recording is read by your browser's own voice, which differs between browsers and devices (Edge offers natural voices; Voice tries another). Nothing is sent anywhere.
  • The sounds are made in your browser for atmosphere: the hiss of the set, church bells, a ship's bell in harbour towns, horses on busy streets, birds in the country and a drum under war news. They are not recordings of the time. New York's police siren is a deliberate anachronism.
