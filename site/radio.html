Radio

Tune to a year between 1770 and 1810 and listen to what its newspapers printed, one morning after another. The radio starts on today's date in the year on the dial. Recorded voices read the passages aloud (your browser's own voice reads any passage without a recording); nothing is sent anywhere.

What you hear

  • The words are passages the newspapers printed on that date, as the archive transcribed them: one passage from each paper that day. Reviewed passages (1776, 1793, 1797, 1807 and 1808) come first; they were checked against the page scans, and their notes record misreadings, but the words read aloud are still the machine transcription (in 1776 you hear “slaves” where the papers printed “staves”). Matched passages were found automatically and not checked.
  • The order: each day starts with a greeting to the town of the first paper. Then the radio moves to the next day with surviving pages. Days with no surviving page are skipped, which is a gap in the archive, not silence in the town.
  • The voices were recorded once, when the site was built, with Kokoro, an open speech model: two announcers who take turns, and nine readers, each newspaper keeping one. They are modern and synthetic, not voices of the time. A passage without a recording is read by your browser's own voice, which differs between browsers and devices (Edge offers natural voices; Voice tries another).
  • The sounds are made in your browser for atmosphere: the hiss of the set, church bells, a ship's bell in harbour towns, horses on busy streets, birds in the country and a drum under war news. They are not recordings of the time. New York's police siren is a deliberate anachronism.
