1 · The source

A thin sky is a gap in the archive, not silence

Only 5 newspapers survive for 1784 and 2 for each of 1785 and 1786, against 10 in 1787 and 10 to 17 a year after 1800, and the stories in the sky nearly vanish with them. The low years show what was kept, not how much was printed.

Newspapers that survive, each year
Stories in the sky, by year of first printing (each row its own scale)

1784–1786: less than a fifth of a typical year's storiesother years

How sureCounts from the dataset itself: 77,793 pages of 110 newspapers. A fact about the source, not a test.
What it does not sayNothing about what those papers printed, only how much of them survives. A full year is full because more papers survive, not because more happened.

2 · The source

About one passage in five has a misreading that changes a fact

The archive’s machine transcriptions are good enough to find stories, not to quote them. In a random sample of 200 matched stories, 35 of the 187 passages found on their scans (19%) had a misreading that changes a fact by the reviewers’ own calls, and 64 (34%) under one strict rule for all. In 1776 the machine read “slaves” in 8 of the 9 printings of the resolution opening the ports, where the papers printed “ſtaves”.

187 passages checked against their scans

faithfulminor errorsa misreading that changes a fact: a number, a date, a name or a word with another meaning

How sureA random sample (seed 20261108) of 47,601 matched stories, read against the scans by eight reviewers under one written rule, with a blind second reader for 30 of them. All 187 passages found were on their pages. The range runs from the reviewers’ own calls to the strict rule.
What it does not sayWhich passages are wrong: it is a rate over a sample, not a mark on each passage. The automatic check that every printing gets (is the passage in both transcriptions of its page?) does not predict these errors, so check the scan before quoting.

3 · A story travels

The Declaration reached 14 newspapers in 21 days: the farther, the later

Adopted on 4 July 1776, the Declaration of Independence was first printed in this archive on 6 July in Philadelphia. By 26 July it had appeared in 14 of its newspapers, and the farther a paper’s town from Philadelphia, the later it printed it.

Each dot is one printing: across, its date; up, how far its town is from Philadelphia, as the crow flies
How sureA reviewed story: each printing was found, read and checked against its scan. Distance and delay agree closely: a rank correlation of 0.83 over its 14 newspapers, as travel by post road and ship would make them. It is one story, so it shows how this one travelled.
What it does not sayWho copied whom. A paper may have taken the text from any earlier paper, a handbill or a letter; the dots show when and where each printed it, not the road the text took. The distances are straight lines; the roads and the sea were longer.

4 · Coming back

The embargo came back with every crisis

Stories mentioning an embargo rose in 5 waves: 1794; 1796; 1798; 1801; 1808–1810. Two are American, the embargo of 1794 and the Embargo Act of 1807–1809, whose year 1808 alone holds 267 stories; between them come three European crises.

Share of the year’s stories that mention “embargo” · gold: wave years · dashed outline: too little archive to judge

The three small waves between are European: 1796, Spain’s embargoes on British and Dutch ships; 1798, French embargoes on American ships feared; 1801, Russia and Britain embargo each other’s ships.

How sureA wave year has 3 or more such stories, twice the word’s usual share and P < .05. The same rule finds the war with Tripoli (1801–1805) exactly, both American embargoes, the Algiers crisis and the great fever years; fire, the control, stays flat but for one borderline year.
What it does not sayIt follows the word, not one policy: 1796, 1798 and 1801 are Spanish, French, Russian and British embargoes. It counts reprinted stories, not events.

5 · Seasons

Fever came back each late summer; fire kept no season

63% of fever stories were first printed in July to September. Fires, which were reported all year, are the control: they keep to no month.

Yellow, malignant or prevailing fever · 87 stories · R 0.58
Fire broke out, conflagration… · 126 stories · R 0.05

Share of each kind’s stories first printed in each month · dashed line: no season (8.3% a month)

How sureCircular statistics on the day of first printing: fever R 0.58 (Rayleigh P < .001), fire R 0.05. The archive’s surviving pages are spread evenly over the year (R 0.007), so the season is not made by which months survive.
What it does not sayWhen stories were first printed in this archive, not when epidemics began; and only these three phrases, so a report that says “the sickness” is missed.

6 · Seasons

The year is the only cycle this archive keeps

Scanning every cycle from 6 to 400 days, 7 of the 10 kinds of story tested have their strongest cycle at one year, and none has a weekly or monthly rhythm of its own. A month or half a year shows only as an echo of a sharp yearly date, such as the Fourth of July.

The strongest cycle of each kind, on a scale from 6 to 400 days · gold: at one year
How sureEach kind is compared with the archive’s own stories within each year, which removes the newspapers’ printing days, and against 300 random draws, with the scan of many cycles paid for. The others peak elsewhere: hurricanes at 149.6 days (P 0.01); fire (control) at 8.2 days (P 0.15); prices current at 8.2 days (P 0.06); none convincingly once ten kinds are tested together.
What it does not sayWhy things come back at a season, and nothing about cycles longer than a year, such as elections every four years (below).

7 · Coming back

Every four years, from 1800

Stories about elections peak in the presidential years 1800, 1804 and 1808 (3.3–3.8% of the year’s stories); before 1800 the rhythm is weak (2.3% in 1792, 1.2% in 1796), and 1802 is high too (3.2%).

Share of the year’s stories that mention an election · gold: presidential election years
How sureShares of all stories each year; the other years from 1789 run from 0.5% to 3.2%. Five election years make a short series, so this is a pattern to read, not a tested cycle.
What it does not sayWhich elections: state and congressional elections fill the other years, and the words cannot tell them apart.

Open questions

How much was false news?A correction can be counted; a false report cannot be known as false without a record of the true one. Without that denominator no rate can be given.
Who copied whom?Shared wording shows that papers printed the same text, not where each took it from. The site draws a copying route only where a paper names its source.
Is a share of the papers a share of attention?No. These are shares of what survives and was reprinted; how much of each year survives varies more than tenfold, so finding 1 comes first.
Why do things come back?The data says when: fever in the late summer, the embargo with each crisis. Why is a question for the history behind the papers.
