How it works

Newspaper Night Sky shows what one surviving archive of American newspapers printed, and when. It does not show everything that was printed, or what people thought. Here is how it was made, and what to keep in mind. What it has shown so far, tested, is on Findings; storylines read from the papers are in Stories; how it was made, wrong turns included, is in the Notebook.

The archive

The newspapers come from the Smithsonian's Revolution Crossroads collection, drawn from the Library of Congress's Chronicling America: 77,793 pages from 110 newspaper series, 1770 to 1810. Of these, 77,749 pages have enough readable text to use.

What survived is uneven. In 1785 and 1786 only two series are in the collection, and 1786 has 356 pages against 4,381 for 1806. Many papers appear for a few years and vanish. So the site always shows which pages survive next to what they printed: an empty stretch usually means missing pages, not silence.

The text of each page is a machine transcription of the scan, either a recent AI transcription or older OCR. Both make mistakes. One printed “dismasted”, and the transcription read “distressed”; in another case the AI transcription left out a whole 350-word statement that is plainly on the page. That is why every page links to its scan.

Some records are misfiled, and the site corrects what the scans confirm. The collection files 106 issues of the Kentucky Gazette two years late (most of its 1806 under 1808), so they are placed by the dates on their mastheads. In 133 issues the AI transcription of a page is stored under another page, so those printings link to the scan they transcribe. Every error we found in the archive itself is set out on Findings, for the people who keep it, with tables that list each case.

Matched stories automatic · not checked

Newspapers of this period copied each other constantly: a report printed in Boston would reappear in Portsmouth, Philadelphia and Richmond over the following weeks. When two papers print a long run of the same wording, it is almost certainly the same item. A computer can find those runs across all 41 years.

  1. Each page's text is cut into overlapping five-word fingerprints.
  2. Pages from different newspapers printed within 120 days of each other are compared, and a match needs at least 80 words in the same order with 70% similarity.
  3. Matches that overlap on the same page are joined into one matched story.
  4. Each story gets a title from the text printed just before the shared words, so it starts where the item starts (this works for 94% of stories).

The result: 310,155 matches, joined into 48,270 matched stories with 164,698 printings. About two thirds were printed in exactly two papers; 15,257 reached three or more.

Matched stories are not read or checked one by one. A match can join two neighbouring items on a page; printings in badly damaged text are missed; reprints within the same paper are not counted; and serial documents or long debates can chain together over months (these are marked “long-running”). How far they can be trusted was measured on a sample, below.

How matched stories were checked

Matched stories were found by machine, so they were checked twice: every printing by machine, and a random sample against the page scans (8 November 2026, data release v0.6.0).

Every printing, by machine. Each page has two machine transcriptions: the Library of Congress OCR and a newer AI transcription. For each printing we asked whether its passage also appears in the page's other transcription, measured against how well the two agree on the rest of that page. The pulse page shows the result beside every matched passage.

  • 81% of printings are found in both transcriptions, and 5% partly.
  • 2.3% are missing from the other transcription, although it reads the rest of the page. Those are worth reading on the scan.
  • For the other 11%, the second transcription is too poor, or absent, to compare.
  • 97.7% of stories have at least one printing found in both.

This shows that a passage is on its page. It cannot show that every word was read correctly.

200 stories, against the scans. 200 matched stories were drawn at random. AI reviewers read every printing side by side and compared one printing per story with its page scan, under one written rule; a second reader re-judged 30 of them blind.

  • 86% of the stories are one item in every printing: 91% of those printed in two papers, 75% of those in three or more. The rest mostly join neighbouring paragraphs that papers copied as a block.
  • Every passage that could be located on its scan (187) is printed there.
  • About 1 passage in 5, and up to 1 in 3, contains a misread word that changes a fact or the sense: “persecution” read as “perfection”, “2,500” as “3,500”, “Mr. Smilie” as “Mr. Smith”, and “ſailed” (with the long s) as “failed”. Passages found in both transcriptions were misread about as often as the rest: the machine check compares runs of words, so one wrong word barely moves it.

So the matched stories are real, and most are one item, but nothing should be quoted from a matched transcription without checking the scan. Every printing on the pulse page links to its page. The reviewers were AI assistants, not an independent human reading.

The check also found that the matcher had listed some pages more than once for the same story. Each printing now counts once per page, which lowered the count of printings from 179,966 to 164,698; the number of newspapers per story did not change.

Reviewed stories read · checked against the scans

For five years, stories were also found, read and grouped by hand, and every printing was checked against its page scan. In 1776: 31 stories with 333 printings in 16 papers, from the King's speech of October 1775 and Wilkes's speech in the Commons to the Declaration of Independence, the Staten Island peace conference and Gates on Carleton's retreat. In 1793: 12 stories with 111 printings in 9 papers, from Dumouriez's letter on entering Brussels and his defection to the President's rules on neutrality, Genêt's quarrel with the President and Captain Crabtree's sea serpent; fewer papers survive from 1793, so a story there needed only six papers. In 1797: 21 stories with 107 printings, from Boyd's report of the French fleet at Bantry Bay to the Blount affair and the mutiny on the Hermione. In 1807: 14 stories with 250 printings in 11 papers, from Burr's conspiracy and trial, the Chesapeake affair and Congress called early to Napoleon's bulletins and the armistice between France and Russia. In 1808: 20 stories with 298 printings in 10 papers, from the embargo laws, the charges against General Wilkinson and the news from Algiers to the war in Spain and the conventions in Portugal.

Each printing is labelled by what the text does — a report, an appended challenge, a later account, a translated document — not by whether it is true. The scan checks keep transcription errors apart from real differences between printings: one paper's “65, 55” turned out to be a transcription error for “65, 35”, while another paper really did print a different date. In 1776, every paper printed Congress's resolutions opening the ports with “ſtaves” (staves), and the machine transcription reads “slaves” in eight of the nine. In 1808 it reads “towels” for “fowls” and “Juan” for Junot.

The reading and scan checks so far were done with AI assistants (Codex and Claude Code). An independent human second reader is still to come.

What a pulse shows

On the pulse page, each newspaper is a point on a map, placed roughly by its town. When a paper printed a story, its point pulses on that date. That is the whole claim: newspaper X printed this on date Y.

  • There are no lines showing who copied whom. Most printings do not name their source, so the route a story took is unknown.
  • When a paper prints a story, the papers that printed the same story in the previous 40 days glow again with it. Constellation lines, shown only when you pick a story, group its printings by nearness on the map; they are not routes either.
  • The bars along the timeline show how many papers survive each week. A missing bar is a gap in the archive.
  • The year strip at the top shows how many newspapers survive each year, from 1770 to 1810, with a gold dot for each year checked by hand and a bright line at the date the pulses have reached (the same bars as under the telescope’s timeline). Tap a year, drag across several, or choose All: a view of many years places every newspaper of those years on the map, so the map can be crowded.
  • The stories of the chosen years sit on a shelf above the map, the most widely printed (or the earliest) first, with the reviewed stories of 1776, 1793, 1797, 1807 and 1808 in their own groups. Each reviewed year has its own overview, which colours its stories by three categories named for that year. Topic chips, from the project's topic model, and a find box for the words of a title narrow both the shelf and what the map plays: choose a family or one of its 24 topics, and the map pulses with those stories only. Tour plays one story after another on its own, and waits while you explore. With Radio on, a newspaper speaks when you point at it or pick one of its printings, and dragging across the map makes the papers in that area speak together (see the radio).

The telescope

The telescope shows all 48,289 stories at once, as stars. Time runs into the distance, as when we look at the night sky: the front is “now”, and the further away a star is, the earlier the story. Drift lets time pass slowly. The sky fills the screen; its controls sit in a panel beside it, which folds away for a bigger sky (on a phone, Controls opens it), the time controls sit around the timeline under the sky, and the camera buttons on the sky itself. The timeline has two rows on one time line, each with its own scale: above, the stories in the sky first printed each month; below, how many newspapers survive each year in this archive, with a gold dot for each year checked by hand. These are the same bars as the year strip on Pulses, and both draw the stretch in view as a bright bracket and the moment (“now”, or the date the pulses have reached) as a bright line. Where the lower row is thin, as in 1784–86, the sky is thin because little survives, not because little was printed. Bigger, brighter stars were printed in more newspapers. Where many stars crowd together, as in a busy town, each is drawn fainter, so the crowd reads as separate stars instead of a white blur; a star's size never changes.

The same stars can be arranged three ways: by the town where each story was first printed (Place), by what it is about (Topic), or by the time of year (Orbit). A star's size always means how many newspapers printed the story. Search lights up every story whose title or opening words mention a word or name, in gold; the other stories turn fainter but keep their size, so the matches can be read against everything else.

How a word comes back. The search card says two things about time. First, whether the matches keep to a season, which is tested on the day of the year. Second, whether they come back in waves over the years, for example embargo: 1794; 1796; 1798; 1801; 1808–1810. A wave year has at least three matches, at least twice the word’s usual share of the stories that year, and more than chance would give (P < .05). A gold line over the timeline marks it. Years with less than a fifth of a typical year’s stories are named as too little archive to judge, never as quiet. The rule was checked against known history: it finds the war with Tripoli (1801–1805), both American embargoes, the Algiers crisis and the great fever years, while fire, a control, stays flat but for one borderline year. The extra embargo waves are real European crises (1796, 1798, 1801): the rule follows the word, not one policy. Other cycle lengths were tested too, from a week to a year: the year is the only one this archive supports, because a week shows the newspapers’ printing days and the other peaks are echoes of the year. That is why Orbit keeps the year.

Compare puts up to three searches side by side, each in its own colour: type “vs” between them, for example fever vs ice, or pick a ready-made comparison such as France · Britain · Congress. A white star mentions more than one of them. The timeline then compares each year's share of stories, and Orbit compares the months. A colour here means only that the story's title or opening words contain those words, nothing a model has guessed. One comparison already tells a history: Britain fills the papers of the 1770s, France rises with the 1778 alliance and peaks during the French Revolution and the Quasi-War (45–46% of the stories printed in three or more papers in 1793, 1796 and 1798), Congress peaks in 1789, its first year under the Constitution, and Britain returns in the embargo years. The three colours were checked to stay distinguishable for colour-blind readers. Colour has one control, Region · Search · Off, so a colour on screen only ever means one thing: a search switches it to Search, and choosing Region instead shows where the matching stories were first printed, with all the searches together as the bright stars.

With Radio on, a star speaks when the pointer rests on it: the town and month where its story was first printed, then its opening words, in that town's voice. Rest on another star and the first stops like a record under a hand, with a scratch, so moving from star to star alternates between voices; stars passed over quickly stay silent. A click chooses a star (below), and the chosen story is heard in full; a click on empty sky lets the voices go. The select tool on the sky (or Alt and drag) marks an area: up to five of its most widely printed stories speak together. A search plays as a relay through time: its four most widely printed matches in view, one after another in date order (see the radio).

The sky is plain until you choose a colour: in the Place view, a star's position already says where its story was first printed. Region colours each star by the region where its story was first printed in this archive: violet for New England, aqua for the Middle States, orange for the South and West (Washington, Virginia, Kentucky and South Carolina). They make the shape of the archive visible. In the 1780s and 1790s most stories were first printed in the Middle States; after 1800 most were first printed in the South and West. That reflects which papers survive, not where the news began. The three colours were checked to stay distinguishable for colour-blind readers; the Colour control can show your searches instead, or turn colour off. Every setting can also be given in the address, for example telescope.html?view=topic&lookback=5&angle=side&colour=off&year=1797, so a view can be linked.

One story, found twice, is shown once. Most stories checked by hand were also found by the computer: 85 of the 98 have an automatic twin, the matched story that holds at least half of their printings (six matched stories are the twin of two checked ones, where the computer joined what the reviewers kept apart). The sky draws each such story once, as its checked star, so it holds 48,289 stars rather than 48,368; a link to the twin opens the checked story, and Pulses lists it once. “Checked by hand only” shows just the checked stories; long-running texts, all found automatically, then step aside too.

Point to preview, click to choose. Pointing at a star shows what it is: its title, how it was found, its newspapers and dates, where it was first printed and what it is about. A click chooses it, the same way whatever the radio is doing, and its card stays beside it with three things to do. Hear plays it: the announcer gives the town and the date, then a recorded voice reads it (with the radio off, it plays on its own). Follow it in Pulses opens the story paper by paper; a double click does the same. Trace a word offers up to three words of its title, each with its number of stories: a tap lights every story that mentions it, with its yearly shares and season in the search card. The words offered are those whose stories are spread over the years, or over the months, unlike the archive's own stories (a G-test against all the stars, p < .001), so that a trace shows more than how much of the archive survives; the most different come first. ✕, Esc or a click on empty sky closes the card. While a story is chosen, Drift and the radio's own playing wait, and the address keeps it (?story=), so a link opens the same card. Pulses has the same actions under its map: Hear reads the printing chosen now, and its words to trace open the sky.

The radio

The radio reads the newspapers aloud, as if a set could be tuned to the past. Pick a year on the dial: it starts on today's date in that year and greets the town of the first paper, for example “Good morning, Philadelphia! It is Saturday, the sixth of July, seventeen seventy-six. From the Pennsylvania Evening Post.” Then it reads the passage that paper printed that day, as the archive transcribed it, and moves on to the next paper, and the next day with surviving pages.

  • What it reads: one passage from each paper on each date. Reviewed passages come first (1776, 1793, 1797, 1807 and 1808); among matched passages, those whose transcription the cross-check confirmed, then those printed in the most newspapers. A day with no surviving page is skipped: a gap in the archive, not silence in the town.
  • On Pulses and the telescope, the radio follows what you see: the switch sits at the top right of the header, the same on every page that reads aloud, and stays on as you move between them. Turned on, it opens with strings, church bells, the town's own sound and a greeting, then plays on its own: on Pulses, a printing that has just pulsed (a reviewed passage first, otherwise the title of a widely printed story); in the telescope, the most widely printed stories arriving at "now" as time drifts, or those in view when it stands still. Each story starts with the tuning sound and its place and date. The moment you point, click or select, you take over, and six seconds after you stop, the radio carries on by itself. A newspaper or a star speaks when you activate it. Point at it and it reads its opening words; move on and it stops with a scratch, like a record under a hand, while the next one spins up, so moving quickly across the map alternates between voices. A voice starts once the pointer rests for a moment, so passing quickly over the map stays quiet. Pick a printing, or click a star, and it is read in full (a picked printing reads its passage); Hear does the same with the radio off. Select an area (drag across the Pulses map, or use the select tool or Alt and drag in the telescope) and up to five papers or stories in it speak together. In the telescope, a search plays its four most widely printed matches one after another, in date order. Under a voice, the town's sound is short and rare, so moving quickly still reaches the news. Each voice is placed left or right by where it is on the screen, and the ones on air are marked in gold.
  • The voices: most of what the radio says was recorded once, when the site was built, with Kokoro, an open speech model (Apache 2.0): two announcers for the towns, dates and newspapers, a man and a woman who take turns, and nine readers, five men and four women with American and British voices: each newspaper's passages keep one of them, and each town's titles are shared by two to four of them, women and men alike. Every reviewed passage is recorded whole, and so are the titles of every story printed in four or more newspapers and of the forty most widely printed stories of each year. Your browser only plays these files, so it does almost no work. A reviewed passage sounds clear, like a near station; a title comes through the radio band, like a distant one. The voices are modern and synthetic, not voices of the time. On Pulses and the telescope, a story without a recording gets the announcer's introduction (its town or paper and its date), and its opening words appear on air. On the Radio page, passages without a recording are read by your browser's own voice. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing is generated as you listen.
  • Misreadings are heard too. The words read aloud are the archive's machine transcription. Reviewed passages were checked against the scans, and their notes record the misreadings, but their text is not corrected. For example, the 1776 resolution opening the ports is read with “slaves” where the papers printed “staves”.
  • The sounds are made in the browser for atmosphere: a long, soft string chord when the radio is first turned on, a faint hiss of the set, a tuning whistle between passages and as the dial turns, a scratch when a voice is stopped or the station changes, church bells for a new day, a ship's bell in harbour towns, horses on busy streets, birds in the country and a drum under war news. They are not recordings of the time. The town sounds ring as they would between buildings, with a few echoes off the facades and a long tail. The police siren in New York is a deliberate anachronism, kept for the feeling of a city morning: it is heard a few streets away, mostly through its echoes, over a faint hum of city traffic with a car passing now and then, and at most once in 45 seconds.

Stories

Stories puts the printed pieces of one affair in order, as the newspapers printed them. The first follows the Chesapeake affair of 1807: eleven pieces in six acts, from the account of the attack to the verdicts of November, each a reviewed story of its own.

  • What each piece shows: what it says; its opening as one paper printed it, beside the scan of that page; how the papers set it, from their headings and lead-ins to the words they changed; and every paper that printed it, with a link to follow it in Pulses.
  • What it does not say: who copied whom, unless a paper names its source, and anything the papers did not print. The short paragraph that opens each act uses only what they printed. Where two voices answered each other in print, as the British commodore and the Mayor of Norfolk did, they are set side by side.
  • How it is checked: each storyline is written by hand, and the site's build checks it against the data release and the scan check every time. Each opening shown comes from a printing whose transcription the scan check found faithful. Every phrase in quotation marks appears in a printing, in the story's reviewed description or in the scan check's notes on that printing. Dates and counts are filled from the data.
  • The machine's misreadings stay in view: where the archive's machine transcription misread a printing, an act shows the scan and both readings, at most one per act. In the Chesapeake storyline, a Norfolk heading, “WAR INEVITABLE!!!”, comes out of the machine as “NECESSARY INFORMATION!!”. Reading that storyline also found a whole issue filed one page off its scans, which led to the correction of 133 issues in the archive.

On Pulses and in the telescope, a story that a storyline tells offers “Read the storyline”.

Exhibition mode

For a large screen, the exhibition plays a loop of seven scenes on its own: the sky of all the stories, two stories travelling (Peace and Plenty and the Declaration of Independence), the seasons of Independence Day and fever, the topics, the gaps in the archive, and the radio. Each scene is one of the pages in its exhibition mode (?exhibit=1: no menus or lists, large type), with a caption and a QR code that opens the same view on a phone. The dots at the top show which scene is playing; tap one to go to it. In the two story scenes, the radio reads the first printing as the story starts. A touch pauses the loop for exploring; a minute without a touch brings it back. Staff keys: F for full screen, Space to pause, the arrows to change scene, M for sound. Browsers play sound only after a first touch; on an unattended screen, start Chrome with --autoplay-policy=no-user-gesture-required.

Watch: on the telescope and on Pulses, the Watch button turns the view you are on into the exhibition's look, in place and full screen, with no controls: the sky drifts through time, or the stories play one after another. Esc, or Exit in the corner, brings the controls back as they were. A link with ?watch=1 opens in it.

Topics

The Topic view uses a model the project trained earlier on the whole archive: it finds 24 groups of words that tend to appear together, such as ship · capt · arrived or french · france · war. Each topic was then named by hand, for example Shipping and ports, and placed in one of seven families from the first study: politics; war, diplomacy and shipping; commerce and property; law, debt and taxes; escape and capture; poetry and reflection; and formats and mixed. Around the ring each topic is a wedge, grouped by family with a gap between families.

A story's topic mix says how much of its wording belongs to each topic. Each star sits in the wedge of its main topic: on the wedge's middle line when the story is about that topic alone, and toward the edge facing its next topic when it mixes topics. Hovering over a star shows its topic, that topic's share of the mix, and the next topic. Stories printed in more newspapers sit toward the rim.

The model goes by vocabulary, not by what an item is, so a fire report that names houses and shops can land among the shop notices. Three topics, marked with a question mark, were unstable when the model was retrained. As a check, different printings of the same story land in the same topic 89% of the time.

Why the families are not colours. We tried colouring the stars by family, folded into news, notices and other. A spot check of matched stories in three or more papers found the news stars were news, but only 2 or 3 of 14 “notices” were notices, and 4 of 14 “other” were poems or essays; the rest were news. Even a strict cut-off left about 4 notices in 10 wrong. Advertisements were paid for in one paper and rarely reprinted, so few matched stories are notices at all. Colour stays with region, which is recorded, not guessed.

Seasons

Some news comes back at the same time every year. A check on the matched stories found clear yearly rhythms for these kinds of story (keyword searches; the archive's surviving pages are spread almost perfectly evenly over the year, so this is not a gap in the collection):

Kind of storyStoriesWhen
Independence Day11981% in July–September, in 25 of 30 years
Thanksgiving25October–January
The President's annual speech or message84November–January, every year it appears
Ice and frozen rivers45Winter and spring
Fever and epidemics122July–September
Congress proceedings1,474December–May, while Congress sits
Fires (for comparison)124No yearly rhythm

A four-year rhythm around presidential elections shows clearly only from 1800. In the telescope's Orbit view, a search shows its months as petals; if the petals all reach the dashed circle, there is no rhythm.

Using the site without a mouse or without sight

  • Keyboard: the first Tab shows Skip to content, which jumps past the menu to the page's own controls. Each single-choice control (speed, look-back, view and the like) is one stop for Tab; the arrow keys move within it, and Home and End go to its ends. The year strip on Pulses works the same way: the arrows move a year, and Shift with an arrow widens the stretch. In the telescope, the up and down arrows travel through time, left and right turn the view, and plus and minus zoom.
  • Screen readers: every control has a name. The telescope's stars are listed as links under the sky (Brightest stories in view), and Pulses announces each printing as you move to it. What the radio reads is also written on screen, with the other papers on air named below it.
  • Motion and sound: with reduced motion set on your device, nothing drifts, glows or plays on its own. Sound never starts until you turn the radio on, and the Radio switch in the header turns it off again.
  • Phones: every page fits a 320-pixel-wide screen without sideways scrolling, and controls are at least 24 pixels tall.

What this cannot tell you

  • Not everything printed. Only what survives in this collection, and only what could be read in its transcriptions.
  • Not public opinion. How often papers printed something is not the same as how much people cared.
  • Not who copied whom. Printing order is not a copying route unless a paper names its source.
  • Not truth. A story printed in thirteen papers can still be wrong; roles describe what a text does, not whether it is accurate.
  • Counts depend on the archive. More surviving pages mean more stories; compare years with care.

Data and code

Everything shown here is built from a versioned data release (currently v0.10.2). New evidence never overwrites old: each round of review or automatic analysis becomes a new release with its own checks, so any view can say which version it uses.
