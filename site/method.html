How it works

Newspaper Night Sky shows what one surviving archive of American newspapers printed, and when. It does not show everything that was printed, or what people thought. Here is how it was made, and what to keep in mind. What it has shown so far, tested, is on Findings; threads read from the papers are in Threads; how it was made, wrong turns included, is in the Notebook.

The archive

The newspapers come from the Smithsonian's Revolution Crossroads collection, drawn from the Library of Congress's Chronicling America: 77,793 pages from 110 newspaper series, 1770 to 1810. Of these, 77,331 pages have enough readable text to use.

What survived is uneven. In 1785 and 1786 only two series are in the collection, and 1786 has 356 pages against 4,381 for 1806. Many papers appear for a few years and vanish. So the site always shows which pages survive next to what they printed: an empty stretch usually means missing pages, not silence.

The text of each page is a machine transcription of the scan, either a recent AI transcription or older OCR. Both make mistakes. One printed “dismasted”, and the transcription read “distressed”; in another case the AI transcription left out a whole 350-word statement that is plainly on the page. That is why every page links to its scan.

Some records are misfiled, and the site corrects what the scans confirm. The collection files 106 issues of the Kentucky Gazette two years late (most of its 1806 under 1808), so they are placed by the dates on their mastheads. In 133 issues the AI transcription of a page is stored under another page, so those printings link to the scan they transcribe. Every error we found in the archive itself is set out on Findings, for the people who keep it, with tables that list each case.

Matched stories automatic · not checked

Newspapers of this period copied each other constantly: a report printed in Boston would reappear in Portsmouth, Philadelphia and Richmond over the following weeks. When two papers print a long run of the same wording, it is almost certainly the same item. A computer can find those runs across all 41 years.

  1. Each page's text is cut into overlapping five-word fingerprints.
  2. Pages from different newspapers printed within 120 days of each other are compared, and a match needs at least 80 words in the same order with 70% similarity.
  3. Matches that overlap on the same page are joined into one matched story.
  4. Each story gets a title from the text printed just before the shared words, so it starts where the item starts (this works for 94% of stories).

The result: 310,155 matches, joined into 48,251 matched stories with 164,149 printings. About two thirds were printed in exactly two papers; 15,257 reached three or more.

Matched stories are not read or checked one by one. A match can join two neighbouring items on a page; printings in badly damaged text are missed; reprints within the same paper are not counted; and serial documents or long debates can chain together over months (these are marked “long-running”). How far they can be trusted was measured on a sample, below.

How matched stories were checked

Matched stories were found by machine, so they were checked twice: every printing by machine, and a random sample against the page scans (8 November 2026, data release v0.6.0).

Every printing, by machine. Each page has two machine transcriptions: the Library of Congress OCR and a newer AI transcription. For each printing we asked whether its passage also appears in the page's other transcription, measured against how well the two agree on the rest of that page. The pulse page shows the result beside every matched passage.

  • 81% of printings are found in both transcriptions, and 5% partly.
  • 2.3% are missing from the other transcription, although it reads the rest of the page. Those are worth reading on the scan.
  • For the other 11%, the second transcription is too poor, or absent, to compare.
  • 97.7% of stories have at least one printing found in both.

This shows that a passage is on its page. It cannot show that every word was read correctly.

200 stories, against the scans. 200 matched stories were drawn at random. AI reviewers read every printing side by side and compared one printing per story with its page scan, under one written rule; a second reader re-judged 30 of them blind.

  • 86% of the stories are one item in every printing: 91% of those printed in two papers, 75% of those in three or more. The rest mostly join neighbouring paragraphs that papers copied as a block.
  • Every passage that could be located on its scan (187) is printed there.
  • About 1 passage in 5, and up to 1 in 3, contains a misread word that changes a fact or the sense: “persecution” read as “perfection”, “2,500” as “3,500”, “Mr. Smilie” as “Mr. Smith”, and “ſailed” (with the long s) as “failed”. Passages found in both transcriptions were misread about as often as the rest: the machine check compares runs of words, so one wrong word barely moves it.

So the matched stories are real, and most are one item, but nothing should be quoted from a matched transcription without checking the scan. Every printing on the pulse page links to its page. The reviewers were AI assistants, not an independent human reading.

The check also found that the matcher had listed some pages more than once for the same story. Each printing now counts once per page, which lowered the count of printings from 179,966 to 164,149; the number of newspapers per story did not change.

Reviewed stories read · checked against the scans

For thirty-eight years, stories were also found, read and grouped story by story, and every printing was checked against its page scan. One rule decided which stories were admitted: every single item printed in nine or more papers, except digests of unrelated paragraphs, and items in seven or eight papers chosen to cover the months and the kinds of news. Where few papers survive the threshold is lower: six papers in 1793, four in 1797. In the thin years, where no single item reaches seven papers, an item is eligible when it was printed by more than half of the papers that survive for its weeks, and by at least four; items printed by every one of them are admitted, and the others are chosen to cover the months and the kinds of news, about as many stories as in other years. All of them have been read this way: 1770–1775, 1777–1783, 1787–1792, 1794–1796, 1798 and 1799; in 1784 no item reaches four papers, and 1785 and 1786 have too few papers to be read.

Who did the work. The author set the questions, the rules for what is admitted and the protocol for checking each printing against its scan, decided every exception, and directed and corrected the work throughout. Following them, AI agents (Anthropic's Claude, and earlier OpenAI's Codex) did the reading: they found and grouped the stories, checked every printing against its page scan, and wrote the Python scripts that cut the scan crops and compare the texts. No person has yet read every printing a second time.

In 1776: 33 stories with 351 printings in 16 papers, from the King's speech of October 1775 and Wilkes's speech in the Commons to the Declaration of Independence, the Staten Island peace conference and Gates on Carleton's retreat. In 1793: 15 stories with 135 printings, from Dumouriez's letter on entering Brussels and his defection to the President's rules on neutrality, Genêt's quarrel with the President and Captain Crabtree's sea serpent; fewer papers survive from 1793, so a story there needed only six papers. In 1797: 29 stories with 140 printings, from Boyd's report of the French fleet at Bantry Bay and Berthier's letter from Milan to the Blount affair, the mutiny on the Hermione and a Wilmington report on the Spanish posts. 1797 was read again by the same rule in November; because only three of its papers survive the whole year, its threshold is four papers. Stories of the first reading that reach fewer are kept, as a recorded exception. In 1800: 16 stories with 182 printings in 11 papers, from the honours Congress paid to Washington and Bonaparte's letter to the King of Great Britain to the siege of Genoa, the first session of Congress in the city of Washington and the convention between France and the United States. In 1801: 15 stories with 160 printings in 11 papers, from Mr. Jefferson's election by the House and his inaugural address to the Bashaw of Tripoli's war on the United States, the remonstrance of the merchants of New Haven and the President's reply, the talks with the Cherokees at South West Point, the census of 1800 and the preliminary articles of peace between Great Britain and France. In 1802: 14 stories with 132 printings in 11 papers, from Mr. Breckenridge's speech on repealing the judiciary act and a Baltimore report that Toussaint "is no more" to the definitive treaty of Amiens, General Leclerc's proclamation on the arrest of Toussaint, the Senate's address to Bonaparte as First Consul for life and Thomas Paine's letters on his return to America. In 1803: 23 stories with 238 printings in 12 papers, from the President's message on the right of deposit at New Orleans and Colonel Despard's speech on the scaffold to Mr. Gascoigne's letter that "War is expected", the treaty and conventions for the cession of Louisiana, a letter from Dublin on the rising and a Raleigh report of gold ore in Cabarrus County. In 1804: 20 stories with 296 printings in 11 papers, from the proclamation of the chiefs of St. Domingo and the taking of Louisiana to the frigate Philadelphia, the impeachment of Judge Chase, the papers on General Hamilton's death after his duel with Colonel Burr and the President's message of November. In 1805: 20 stories with 242 printings in 11 papers, from the impeachment trial of Judge Chase and Napoleon's letter to the King of England to the news of the peace with Tripoli, the news from Lewis and Clark's party at Fort Mandan and Collingwood's dispatch on the action off Cape Trafalgar. In 1806: 33 stories with 464 printings in 12 papers, from the news of Austerlitz and the Treaty of Pressburg to Miranda's expedition, the Leander affair, the return of Lewis and Clark and Burr's enterprise. In 1807: 18 stories with 282 printings in 11 papers, from Burr's conspiracy and trial, the Chesapeake affair and Congress called early to Napoleon's bulletins and the armistice between France and Russia. In 1808: 23 stories with 320 printings in 10 papers, from the embargo laws, the charges against General Wilkinson and the news from Algiers to the war in Spain and the conventions in Portugal. In 1809: 36 stories with 495 printings in 12 papers, from the embargo and non-intercourse laws and Madison's inaugural address to the arrangement with the British minister and its disavowal, Austria's war with France, the treaty of Vienna and the documents sent with the President's message of 29 November. In 1810: 20 stories with 222 printings in 11 papers, from the act of 1 May on commercial intercourse with Great Britain and France and the despatches brought by the frigate John Adams to the Duke of Cadore's letter that the decrees of Berlin and Milan are revoked, the President's proclamation of 2 November, the Natchez report of the taking of the fort of Baton Rouge in West Florida and the President's message of 5 December. In the thin years: 516 stories with 2,800 printings in twenty-four years, from a Williamsburg report of Lord Botetourt's death in 1770 and the news of three tea ships in 1773 to the Boston Port Act, the Congress's petitions to the King, the convention of Saratoga, the surrender at York, the provisional articles of peace, the Constitution and the Virginia convention's ratification of it, General St. Clair's defeat in 1791, the insurrection in the western counties of Pennsylvania in 1794, the treaties with Great Britain and with Spain, in 1798 the envoys' dispatches and the act against sedition, and in 1799 the President's proclamation on the insurrection in Pennsylvania and reports of Buonaparte's defeat near Acre. Year by year: 1770, 3 stories with 12 printings; 1771, 1 with 4; 1772, 11 with 52; 1773, 49 with 208; 1774, 25 with 159; 1775, 24 with 206; 1777, 26 with 186; 1778, 26 with 161; 1779, 27 with 161; 1780, 23 with 142; 1781, 25 with 144; 1782, 30 with 165; 1783, 22 with 109; 1787, 13 with 54; 1788, 7 with 30; 1789, 6 with 24; 1790, 5 with 20; 1791, 18 with 98; 1792, 29 with 146; 1794, 34 with 194; 1795, 21 with 102; 1796, 28 with 116; 1798, 19 with 118; 1799, 44 with 189. A later round added 12 stories to 1776, 1793, 1807 and 1808 that had been held back for framing, among them reports on the Creek and Cherokee nations and news from Cap Français and from Haiti; they were chosen and described by the same rules as every other story. The items that were read and not admitted, 3,279 in all, are listed year by year with their reasons in a list in the repository (also as a table), drawn from each year's ledger. Items that turn on private persons are described there without names.

Each printing is labelled by what the text does — a report, an appended challenge, a later account, a translated document — not by whether it is true. The scan checks keep transcription errors apart from real differences between printings: one paper's “65, 55” turned out to be a transcription error for “65, 35”, while another paper really did print a different date. In 1776, every paper printed Congress's resolutions opening the ports with “ſtaves” (staves), and the machine transcription reads “slaves” in eight of the nine. In 1808 it reads “towels” for “fowls” and “Juan” for Junot.

The reading and scan checks so far were done with AI assistants (Codex and Claude Code). An independent human second reader is still to come.

What a pulse shows

On the pulse page, each newspaper is a point on a map, placed roughly by its town. When a paper printed a story, its point pulses on that date. That is the whole claim: newspaper X printed this on date Y.

  • There are no lines showing who copied whom. Most printings do not name their source, so the route a story took is unknown.
  • When a paper prints a story, the papers that printed the same story in the previous 40 days glow again with it. Constellation lines, shown only when you pick a story, group its printings by nearness on the map; they are not routes either.
  • The bars along the timeline show how many papers survive each week. A missing bar is a gap in the archive.
  • The year strip at the top shows how many newspapers survive each year, from 1770 to 1810, with a gold dot for each reviewed year and a bright line at the date the pulses have reached (the same bars as under the telescope’s timeline). Tap a year, drag across several, or choose All: a view of many years places every newspaper of those years on the map, so the map can be crowded.
  • A line of words above the map says what is playing: which evidence, which years, which stories, how they are arranged and how they are coloured. Tap an underlined part to change it in place; Copy link keeps the view as a link.
  • The stories of the chosen years sit on a shelf that opens from that line (on a first visit it opens by itself for a few seconds, then folds back), the most widely printed (or the earliest) first, with the reviewed stories of 1770–1783 and 1787–1810 in their own groups. Each reviewed year has its own overview, which colours its stories by three categories named for that year. Topic chips, from the project's topic model, and a find box for the words of a title narrow both the shelf and what the map plays: choose a family or one of its 24 topics, and the map pulses with those stories only. Tour plays one story after another on its own, and waits while you explore. With Radio on, a newspaper speaks when you point at it or pick one of its printings, and dragging across the map makes the papers in that area speak together (see the radio).
  • Music: the Music switch, beside the radio's, plays what the map shows as music while it plays, in one of three sounds chosen under Sound: Wood (wooden bars for the surviving issues, plucked strings for the printings), Piano (a recorded grand piano over soft strings, its line rising in the busier weeks) or Concerto (the piano with a flute and a clarinet). With the music on, one year, or a story in it, plays at the music's own pace: at Normal, a week of printing in two seconds. Every note is a surviving issue or a printing; fuller strings mean more surviving newspapers, not more news, and a thin year sounds thin. Piano and Concerto take their shape from the slow movement of Rachmaninoff's Second Piano Concerto, but no melody of his is used. On the Listen page, the Music tab plays it alone, as loud as the voices would be, with its own picture: each newspaper a row by latitude, its surviving issues dots that glow as they sound. The notes are made in your browser from recordings: Piano: Salamander Grand Piano by Alexander Holm (CC BY 3.0); flute and clarinet from the VSCO 2 Community Edition orchestra by Versilian Studios (CC0).

The telescope

The telescope shows all 48,385 stories at once, as stars. Time runs into the distance, as when we look at the night sky: the front is “now”, and the further away a star is, the earlier the story. Drift lets time pass slowly. The sky fills the screen. A line of words above it says what the sky shows: which evidence, which years, which stories, how they are arranged and how they are coloured. Tap an underlined part to change it in place; Copy link keeps the view as a link. The time controls sit around the timeline under the sky, and the camera buttons on the sky itself. The timeline has two rows on one time line, each with its own scale: above, the stories in the sky first printed each month; below, how many newspapers survive each year in this archive, with a gold dot for each reviewed year. These are the same bars as the year strip on Pulses, and both draw the stretch in view as a bright bracket and the moment (“now”, or the date the pulses have reached) as a bright line. Where the lower row is thin, as in 1784–86, the sky is thin because little survives, not because little was printed. Bigger, brighter stars were printed in more newspapers. Where many stars crowd together, as in a busy town, the brightest shows where they overlap, so the crowd reads as separate stars instead of a white blur. Reviewed stars, every printing checked against its scan, are warm white; stars found by shared wording are a cool grey. Place, Topic and Orbit, the three arrangements, sit on the sky too. Ctrl + scroll (or a pinch) zooms the sky; a plain scroll moves the page.

The same stars can be arranged three ways: by the town where each story was first printed (Place), by what it is about (Topic), or by the time of year (Orbit). A star's size always means how many newspapers printed the story. Search lights up every story whose title or opening words mention a word or name, in gold; the other stories turn fainter but keep their size, so the matches can be read against everything else.

How a word comes back. The search card says two things about time. First, whether the matches keep to a season, which is tested on the day of the year. Second, whether they come back in waves over the years, for example embargo: 1794; 1796; 1798; 1801; 1808–1810. A wave year has at least three matches, at least twice the word’s usual share of the stories that year, and more than chance would give (P < .05). A gold line over the timeline marks it. Years with less than a fifth of a typical year’s stories are named as too little archive to judge, never as quiet. The rule was checked against known history: it finds the war with Tripoli (1801–1805), both American embargoes, the Algiers crisis and the great fever years, while fire, a control, stays flat but for one borderline year. The extra embargo waves are real European crises (1796, 1798, 1801): the rule follows the word, not one policy. Other cycle lengths were tested too, from a week to a year: the year is the only one this archive supports, because a week shows the newspapers’ printing days and the other peaks are echoes of the year. That is why Orbit keeps the year.

Compare puts up to three searches side by side, each in its own colour: type “vs” between them, for example fever vs ice, or pick a ready-made comparison such as France · Britain · Congress. A white star mentions more than one of them. The timeline then compares each year's share of stories, and Orbit compares the months. A colour here means only that the story's title or opening words contain those words, nothing a model has guessed. One comparison already tells a history: Britain fills the papers of the 1770s, France rises with the 1778 alliance and peaks during the French Revolution and the Quasi-War (45–46% of the stories printed in three or more papers in 1793, 1796 and 1798), Congress peaks in 1789, its first year under the Constitution, and Britain returns in the embargo years. The three colours were checked to stay distinguishable for colour-blind readers. Colour has one control, Region · Search · Off, so a colour on screen only ever means one thing: a search switches it to Search, and choosing Region instead shows where the matching stories were first printed, with all the searches together as the bright stars.

With Radio on, a star speaks when the pointer rests on it: the town and month where its story was first printed, then its opening words, in that town's voice. Rest on another star and the first stops like a record under a hand, with a scratch, so moving from star to star alternates between voices; stars passed over quickly stay silent. A click chooses a star (below), and the chosen story is heard in full; a click on empty sky lets the voices go. The select tool on the sky (or Alt and drag) marks an area: up to five of its most widely printed stories speak together. A search plays as a relay through time: its four most widely printed matches in view, one after another in date order (see the radio).

The sky is plain until you choose a colour: in the Place view, a star's position already says where its story was first printed. Region colours each star by the region where its story was first printed in this archive: violet for New England, aqua for the Middle States, orange for the South and West (Washington, Virginia, Kentucky and South Carolina). They make the shape of the archive visible. In the 1780s and 1790s most stories were first printed in the Middle States; after 1800 most were first printed in the South and West. That reflects which papers survive, not where the news began. The three colours were checked to stay distinguishable for colour-blind readers; the Colour control can show your searches instead, or turn colour off. Every setting can also be given in the address, for example telescope.html?view=topic&lookback=5&angle=side&colour=off&year=1797, so a view can be linked.

One story, found twice, is shown once. Most reviewed stories were also found by the computer: 781 of the 831 have an automatic twin, the matched story that holds at least half of their printings (49 matched stories are the twin of more than one checked story, where the computer joined what the reviewers kept apart). The sky draws each such story once, as its checked star, so it holds 48,385 stars rather than 49,082; a link to the twin opens the checked story, and Pulses lists it once. “Reviewed only” shows just the reviewed stories; long-running texts, all found automatically, then step aside too.

Point to preview, click to choose. Pointing at a star shows what it is: its title, how it was found, its newspapers and dates, where it was first printed and what it is about. A click chooses it, the same way whatever the radio is doing, and its card stays beside it with three things to do. Hear plays it: the announcer gives the town and the date, then a recorded voice reads it (with the radio off, it plays on its own). Follow it in Pulses opens the story paper by paper; a double click does the same. Trace a word offers up to three words of its title, each with its number of stories: a tap lights every story that mentions it, with its yearly shares and season in the search card. The words offered are those whose stories are spread over the years, or over the months, unlike the archive's own stories (a G-test against all the stars, p < .001), so that a trace shows more than how much of the archive survives; the most different come first. ✕, Esc or a click on empty sky closes the card. While a story is chosen, Drift and the radio's own playing wait, and the address keeps it (?story=), so a link opens the same card. Pulses has the same actions under its map: Hear reads the printing chosen now, and its words to trace open the sky.

The radio

The radio reads the newspapers aloud, as if a set could be tuned to the past. Pick a year on the dial: it starts on today's date in that year and greets the town of the first paper, for example “Good morning, Philadelphia! It is Saturday, the sixth of July, seventeen seventy-six. From the Pennsylvania Evening Post.” Then it reads the passage that paper printed that day, as the archive transcribed it, and moves on to the next paper, and the next day with surviving pages.

  • What it reads: one passage from each paper on each date. Reviewed passages come first (1770–1783 and 1787–1810); among matched passages, those whose transcription the cross-check confirmed, then those printed in the most newspapers. A day with no surviving page is skipped: a gap in the archive, not silence in the town.
  • On Pulses and the telescope, the radio follows what you see: the switch sits at the top right of the header, the same on Telescope and Pulses, and stays on as you move between them; Listen has its own power button. Turned on, it opens with strings, church bells, the town's own sound and a greeting, then plays on its own: on Pulses, a printing that has just pulsed (a reviewed passage first, otherwise the title of a widely printed story); in the telescope, the most widely printed stories arriving at "now" as time drifts, or those in view when it stands still. Each story starts with the tuning sound and its place and date. The moment you point, click or select, you take over, and six seconds after you stop, the radio carries on by itself. A newspaper or a star speaks when you activate it. Point at it and it reads its opening words; move on and it stops with a scratch, like a record under a hand, while the next one spins up, so moving quickly across the map alternates between voices. A voice starts once the pointer rests for a moment, so passing quickly over the map stays quiet. Pick a printing, or click a star, and it is read in full (a picked printing reads its passage); Hear does the same with the radio off. Select an area (drag across the Pulses map, or use the select tool or Alt and drag in the telescope) and up to five papers or stories in it speak together. In the telescope, a search plays its four most widely printed matches one after another, in date order. Under a voice, the town's sound is short and rare, so moving quickly still reaches the news. Each voice is placed left or right by where it is on the screen, and the ones on air are marked in gold.
  • The voices: most of what the radio says was recorded once, when the site was built, with Kokoro, an open speech model (Apache 2.0): two announcers for the towns, dates and newspapers, a man and a woman who take turns, and nine readers, five men and four women with American and British voices: each newspaper's passages keep one of them, and each town's titles are shared by two to four of them, women and men alike. Every reviewed passage is recorded whole, and so are the titles of every story printed in four or more newspapers and of the forty most widely printed stories of each year. Your browser only plays these files, so it does almost no work. A reviewed passage sounds clear, like a near station; a title comes through the radio band, like a distant one. The voices are modern and synthetic, not voices of the time. On Pulses and the telescope, a story without a recording gets the announcer's introduction (its town or paper and its date), and its opening words appear on air. On the Radio page, passages without a recording are read by your browser's own voice. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing is generated as you listen.
  • Misreadings are heard too. The words read aloud are the archive's machine transcription. Reviewed passages were checked against the scans, and their notes record the misreadings, but their text is not corrected. For example, the 1776 resolution opening the ports is read with “slaves” where the papers printed “staves”.
  • The sounds are made in the browser for atmosphere: a long, soft string chord when the radio is first turned on, a faint hiss of the set, a tuning whistle between passages and as the dial turns, a scratch when a voice is stopped or the station changes, church bells for a new day, a ship's bell in harbour towns, horses on busy streets, birds in the country and a drum under war news. They are not recordings of the time. The town sounds ring as they would between buildings, with a few echoes off the facades and a long tail. The police siren in New York is a deliberate anachronism, kept for the feeling of a city morning: it is heard a few streets away, mostly through its echoes, over a faint hum of city traffic with a car passing now and then, and at most once in 45 seconds.

Threads

Threads: each thread follows one affair through several reviewed stories, its printed pieces in the order the newspapers printed them, and the threads are listed by year. A thread follows its affair across the turn of a year when the affair does, and each piece is read in the reviewed data of its own year. Boston and the Congress in 1774: the act that shut the port of Boston, two bills for Massachusetts Bay, the governor's proclamation, the Suffolk resolves, the Congress's resolutions, Association and declaration, the governor's answer and the province's thanks to the other colonies. The King and Congress in 1776 sets the documents of both sides in the order American readers met them, from the King's speech of October 1775 to his commissioners' declaration of September 1776. The King's commissioners and Congress in 1778: the draught bills and Congress's answer, the news of a treaty with France, and the declarations and manifestos of both sides. From the Cowpens to York in 1781: the generals' letters and proclamations, from both sides, to the surrender. The envoys at Paris in 1798: the President's messages on their dispatches, the acts and resolutions of that summer, Mr. Gerry's letters with the French minister, and the President's speech in December. The Chesapeake affair of 1807: eleven pieces in six acts, from the account of the attack to the verdicts of November. The embargo, 1808–1809: the law and the acts that tightened it, letters and reports on how it worked, the President's answers to a legislature and a town, a committee's report to the House in November, and in 1809 the act to enforce it, a Massachusetts Senate committee's report against that act, and the act of non-intercourse that took its place. The Erskine arrangement of 1809: notes exchanged with the British minister in April and trade with Britain reopened, the agreement disavowed in London, and the ban restored by the President's proclamation of 9 August.

  • The chart at its head: each reviewed story of the thread, numbered, by the day it was first printed, on the same time line as the chart of its pieces below, and by how many newspapers printed it, joined in that order; the line shows order, not cause. The other stories of those months in three or more newspapers are faint dots, in the telescope's colours.
  • What each piece shows: what it says; its opening as one paper printed it, beside the scan of that page; how the papers set it, from their headings and lead-ins to the words they changed; and every paper that printed it, with a link to follow it in Pulses.
  • What it does not say: who copied whom, unless a paper names its source, and anything the papers did not print. The short paragraph that opens each act uses only what they printed. Where two voices answered each other in print, as the British commodore and the Mayor of Norfolk did, they are set side by side.
  • How it is checked: each thread is written for its affair, not generated, and the site's build checks it against the data release and the scan check every time. Each opening shown comes from a printing whose transcription the scan check found faithful. Every phrase in quotation marks appears in a printing, in the story's reviewed description or in the scan check's notes on that printing. Dates and counts are filled from the data.
  • The machine's misreadings stay in view: where the archive's machine transcription misread a printing, an act shows the scan and both readings, at most one per act. In the Chesapeake thread, a Norfolk heading, “WAR INEVITABLE!!!”, comes out of the machine as “NECESSARY INFORMATION!!”. Reading that thread also found a whole issue filed one page off its scans, which led to the correction of 133 issues in the archive.

On Pulses and in the telescope, a story that a thread tells offers “Read the thread”.

Exhibition mode

For a large screen, the exhibition plays a loop of seven scenes on its own: the sky of all the stories, two stories travelling (Peace and Plenty and the Declaration of Independence), the seasons of Independence Day and fever, the topics, the gaps in the archive, and the radio. Each scene is one of the pages in its exhibition mode (?exhibit=1: no menus or lists, large type), with a caption and a QR code that opens the same view on a phone. The dots at the top show which scene is playing; tap one to go to it. In the two story scenes, the radio reads the first printing as the story starts. A touch pauses the loop for exploring; a minute without a touch brings it back. Staff keys: F for full screen, Space to pause, the arrows to change scene, M for sound. Browsers play sound only after a first touch; on an unattended screen, start Chrome with --autoplay-policy=no-user-gesture-required.

Watch: on the telescope and on Pulses, the Watch button turns the view you are on into the exhibition's look, in place and full screen, with no controls: the sky drifts through time, or the stories play one after another. Esc, or Exit in the corner, brings the controls back as they were. A link with ?watch=1 opens in it.

Topics

The Topic view uses a model the project trained earlier on the whole archive: it finds 24 groups of words that tend to appear together, such as ship · capt · arrived or french · france · war. Each topic is named for what the stories in it mostly are, for example Ships and the sea, and placed in one of seven families: politics; war, diplomacy and shipping; commerce and property; law, debt and taxes; people sought; poems, anecdotes and reflections; and letters and mixed. Around the ring each topic is a wedge, grouped by family with a gap between families. Two of the 24 topics hold the same estate and debt notices and share one wedge, Estates and debts, so the ring has 23 wedges.

A story's topic mix says how much of its wording belongs to each topic. Each star sits in the wedge of its main topic: on the wedge's middle line when the story is about that topic alone, and toward the edge facing its next topic when it mixes topics. Hovering over a star shows its topic with the topic's three most typical words, that topic's share of the mix, and the next topic. Stories printed in more newspapers sit toward the rim.

The model goes by vocabulary, not by what an item is. A check of 810 reprinted stories, read without knowing their topics, found that a topic's name fitted about half of the stories in its wedge: the topics had been named from whole pages, where advertisements are common, while reprinted stories are mostly news. The names were then changed to those that fitted best in the same kind of test (12 November 2026), and two wedges whose stories share only place names or personal names are called Mixed. Even so, many stories in a wedge are about something else, so Pulses names topics only for groups, not on single stories. Some names fit mainly one period: most notices seeking people, horse notices and estate notices in their wedges are from the 1770s, and the hover card says so. Three topics, marked with a question mark, were unstable when the model was retrained. As a check, different printings of the same story land in the same topic 89% of the time.

Why the families are not colours. We tried colouring the stars by family, folded into news, notices and other. A spot check of matched stories in three or more papers found the news stars were news, but only 2 or 3 of 14 “notices” were notices, and 4 of 14 “other” were poems or essays; the rest were news. Even a strict cut-off left about 4 notices in 10 wrong. Advertisements were paid for in one paper and rarely reprinted, so few matched stories are notices at all. Colour stays with region, which is recorded, not guessed.

Seasons

Some news comes back at the same time every year. A check on the matched stories found clear yearly rhythms for these kinds of story (keyword searches; the archive's surviving pages are spread almost perfectly evenly over the year, so this is not a gap in the collection):

A four-year rhythm around presidential elections shows clearly only from 1800. In the telescope's Orbit view, a search shows its months as petals; if the petals all reach the dashed circle, there is no rhythm.

Using the site without a mouse or without sight

  • Keyboard: the first Tab shows Skip to content, which jumps past the menu to the page's own controls. Each single-choice control (speed, look-back, view and the like) is one stop for Tab; the arrow keys move within it, and Home and End go to its ends. The year strip on Pulses works the same way: the arrows move a year, and Shift with an arrow widens the stretch. In the telescope, the up and down arrows travel through time, left and right turn the view, and plus and minus zoom.
  • Screen readers: every control has a name. The telescope's stars are listed as links under the sky (Brightest stories in view), and Pulses announces each printing as you move to it. What the radio reads is also written on screen, with the other papers on air named below it.
  • Motion and sound: with reduced motion set on your device, nothing drifts, glows or plays on its own. Sound never starts until you turn the radio or the music on, and their switches in the header turn them off again.
  • Phones: every page fits a 320-pixel-wide screen without sideways scrolling, and controls are at least 24 pixels tall.

What this cannot tell you

  • Not everything printed. Only what survives in this collection, and only what could be read in its transcriptions.
  • Not public opinion. How often papers printed something is not the same as how much people cared.
  • Not who copied whom. Printing order is not a copying route unless a paper names its source.
  • Not every voice. No paper in this archive was printed by Native nations, by enslaved people or in Haiti. They speak in it mostly through others, in reports, letters and advertisements, and in their own words only where an American printer chose to reprint them.
  • Not truth. A story printed in thirteen papers can still be wrong; roles describe what a text does, not whether it is accurate.
  • Counts depend on the archive. More surviving pages mean more stories; compare years with care.
