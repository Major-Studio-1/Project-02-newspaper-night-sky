Listen

Tune to a year between 1770 and 1810 and listen to what its newspapers printed, one morning after another. The radio starts on today's date in the year on the dial. Recorded voices read the passages aloud (your browser's own voice reads any passage without a recording); nothing is sent anywhere.

Music from the newspapers that survive, with no words: every note is a surviving issue or a printing of a story, and the picture shows them as they sound. Choose a year or one of its stories and press Play; at Normal a week of printing passes in two seconds.

One story

Choose one and every printing of it plays; choose it again to go back to the year.

PressesEach surviving issue is a grey dot and a soft wooden note, its pitch the town's latitude, south low and north high and a step of the piano's left hand, which rocks through each bar's chord and climbs higher when more newspapers print; struck, it glows. A day with no surviving issue is a rest.
StoriesA printing of a story is a ring, gold if it was checked against the scans and blue if it was found by shared wording; a plucked string sounds it; the piano's right hand sings it, by steps, higher in the busier weeks, a flute opening each rise and a clarinet answering after its peak. The papers that printed the story in the 40 days before ring with it.
StringsSoft strings hold the season's chordeach bar's chord, leaning to tension while the weeks get busier and resolving as they quieten, and the cellos join in the busiest weeks. They are fuller when more newspapers survive around now, not when there is more news.
Air and New YearA faint breath under everything, so a gap in the archive sounds like a gap, not like the music stopping; a soft druma stroke of the timpani on each 1 January. Piano: Salamander Grand Piano by Alexander Holm (CC BY 3.0); flute and clarinet: VSCO 2 Community Edition (CC0). How the music works.

What you hear

  • The words are passages the newspapers printed on that date, as the archive transcribed them: one passage from each paper that day. Reviewed passages (1776, 1793, 1797, 1800, 1801, 1802, 1803, 1804, 1805, 1806, 1807, 1808, 1809 and 1810) come first; they were checked against the page scans, and their notes record misreadings, but the words read aloud are still the machine transcription (in 1776 you hear “slaves” where the papers printed “staves”). Matched passages were found automatically and not checked.
  • The order: each day starts with a greeting to the town of the first paper. Then the radio moves to the next day with surviving pages. Days with no surviving page are skipped, which is a gap in the archive, not silence in the town.
  • The voices were recorded once, when the site was built, with Kokoro, an open speech model: two announcers who take turns, and nine readers, each newspaper keeping one. They are modern and synthetic, not voices of the time. A passage without a recording is read by your browser's own voice, which differs between browsers and devices (Edge offers natural voices; Voice tries another).
  • The sounds are made in your browser for atmosphere: the hiss of the set, church bells, a ship's bell in harbour towns, horses on busy streets, birds in the country and a drum under war news. They are not recordings of the time. New York's police siren is a deliberate anachronism.
